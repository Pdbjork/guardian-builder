Working title

Educating for Value Creation in the Age of Agentic AI: A Participatory Action Research Study of Global Citizenship, Community-Based Bridge Programs, and Threshold-Based Pathways Beyond Compulsory Schooling

Core thesis

This dissertation argues that the advent of agentic artificial intelligence requires a fundamental redesign of secondary education. If AI systems increasingly perform routine cognitive work, personalize learning, automate administrative tasks, generate content, and reshape workforce demands, then schools can no longer justify themselves primarily as systems for content delivery, compliance, credential accumulation, or seat-time completion.

Instead, education must be reorganized around value creation, human agency, ethical judgment, community contribution, and global citizenship. Drawing on Daisaku Ikeda’s conception of global citizenship as wisdom, courage, and compassion, this study examines how community-based bridge programs can help students move from compulsory schooling into supported, threshold-based pathways toward college, apprenticeship, work, civic participation, and meaningful adult life.

Recent scholarship on AI and education strengthens this argument by showing that AI should not be understood merely as a tool-adoption problem. AI is a social, ethical, institutional, and human-development problem. The more powerful AI becomes, the more education must become explicitly humanizing, relational, ethical, and value-creating.

Purpose of the study

The purpose of this study is to investigate how community-based bridge programs can help reimagine compulsory secondary education for an era shaped by agentic AI, unstable labor markets, and urgent demands for ethical global citizenship. The study explores whether a threshold-based model of educational transition can better prepare students for college, career, apprenticeship, civic participation, and human flourishing than the traditional age-graded, seat-time model of schooling.

Rationale and significance

Secondary education was built for a world in which schools served as primary gateways to knowledge, credentials, workforce sorting, and civic preparation. That world is being destabilized. Agentic AI systems can already perform forms of planning, tutoring, drafting, coding, analysis, coordination, and task execution that were once treated as distinctly human knowledge-work capacities.

This shift does not make education less important. It makes humanistic education more important. If AI can increasingly generate answers, schools must focus more deeply on helping students ask meaningful questions, evaluate systems critically, build ethical judgment, collaborate across difference, and create value in their communities.

Emerging literature warns against treating this moment as a simple question of classroom efficiency. AI is not merely a faster tutor, grading assistant, or content generator. It is a social technology that reorganizes the conditions under which students learn, teachers teach, institutions govern, and communities imagine the future. For that reason, the central question is not only “How can schools use AI?” but rather: What kind of human beings and communities should education cultivate in a world where intelligent systems increasingly mediate knowledge, work, and social life?

Conceptual backbone: wisdom, courage, and compassion

This dissertation uses Ikeda’s “Thoughts on Education for Global Citizenship” as its conceptual backbone. In that lecture, Ikeda presents global citizenship not as travel, multilingualism, or elite cosmopolitan identity, but as an ethical orientation rooted in ordinary people’s concern for the peace and prosperity of the world. He identifies three essential elements: wisdom, courage, and compassion.

  • Wisdom refers to the ability to perceive interconnection among self, community, technology, economy, ecology, and global society.
  • Courage refers to the ability to engage difference, confront injustice, take intellectual risks, and adapt to unfamiliar futures.
  • Compassion refers to the ability to maintain imaginative empathy, especially toward those harmed by poverty, exclusion, technological displacement, racialized discipline, or institutional neglect.

Agentic AI adds a new dimension to this framework. Students now need wisdom to understand human-AI interdependence, courage to adapt to new forms of work and learning, and compassion to ensure that technological change serves human dignity rather than deepening inequality.

Expanded review of relevant literature

The literature most aligned with this dissertation converges around five claims: AI is a social technology rather than a neutral tool; AI-era education must remain human-centered; ethics cannot be an add-on to technical implementation; teachers and community partners remain central; and equity depends on local context, governance, and participatory design.

Humanizing AI in education

Humburg and colleagues’ symposium on Humanizing AI for Education emphasizes the need to integrate human-centered learning theories into the design and teaching of AI. The symposium identifies a tension between technocentric approaches, which foreground effectiveness and scale, and human-centered approaches, which foreground the ethical, social, and pedagogical consequences of AI in classrooms and communities (Humburg et al., 2025). This work supports the dissertation’s claim that AI integration should be judged by whether it deepens student agency, teacher judgment, dialogue, and meaningful learning, not simply by whether it improves efficiency.

Alpini and Kanwal (2025) make a similar argument through the language of “AI with a human face.” Their interdisciplinary review argues that AI may transform education for Gen Z and Gen Alpha without eroding its human core if it is grounded in presence, empathy, shared agency, democratic dialogue, intercultural understanding, and creative thinking. Their conclusion that human-centered AI requires ethical governance, equitable access, and pedagogical courage closely parallels this dissertation’s use of wisdom, courage, and compassion as educational capacities for the age of agentic AI.

AI as social technology

Bámaca-López and de la Vega (2026) provide one of the strongest theoretical anchors for this project by framing artificial intelligence as a social technology in education. They argue that AI should be guided by beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability. Their central warning is that if these principles are not applied with a broad vision of human needs, technological marginalization will preserve and intensify social gaps in pedagogical models. This supports the dissertation’s claim that AI literacy cannot be reduced to tool use. Students must understand AI as part of a sociotechnical system involving power, design, governance, human need, and social justice.

Cesaroni (2024) extends this sociotechnical critique through the lens of inclusion. Her analysis of policy guidance and technological mediation warns that inclusion can be collapsed into mere integration or techno-solutionism. This distinction is crucial for the present study. A school may provide access to AI tools while still failing to create belonging, agency, recognition, or justice. A threshold-based pathway model must therefore ask not only whether students can operate AI systems, but whether AI-mediated learning environments help them experience dignity, participation, and real opportunity.

Ethics, governance, and unintended consequences

Holmes and colleagues (2021) argue that ethical intentions in artificial intelligence in education are not sufficient. AIED must explicitly address fairness, accountability, transparency, bias, autonomy, agency, inclusion, pedagogical ethics, and unintended consequences. Their distinction between “doing ethical things” and “doing things ethically” is especially important for this dissertation. A bridge program may aim to help students, but it must still ask whether its tools, data practices, recommendations, and institutional effects are ethical.

Sangwa (2025) moves from ethical principle toward governance design. His Dynamic AI Governance in Education framework identifies recurring policy needs around academic integrity, data privacy, teacher capacity, equity, and AI literacy. The framework’s emphasis on multi-stakeholder co-creation and continuous improvement supports this dissertation’s participatory action research design. If readiness thresholds are to replace or supplement seat-time requirements, those thresholds must be governed transparently and collaboratively: students, educators, families, community partners, and institutions must all have a voice in defining what readiness means.

Teacher agency and institutional readiness

El Samaty and Al Aqra (2026) examine AI integration across Europe, Asia, and Africa and show that classroom implementation depends on policy context, teacher agency, professional development, and ethical guidance. They argue that teachers are not passive users of technology; they are frontline decision-makers who must balance automation with professional judgment, curriculum, student well-being, privacy, bias, empathy, fairness, and inclusivity. This supports the dissertation’s insistence that AI-era redesign cannot bypass teachers. Community-based bridge programs should work with educators, not around them.

Washington (2026) sharpens the equity argument by describing the absence of AI curriculum as a new mechanism for producing “left behind” students. She compares the emerging AI literacy divide to the COVID-19 digital divide and argues that the gap is curricular and institutional, not merely technological. This claim is directly relevant to threshold-based pathways. If students are expected to navigate college, careers, apprenticeships, civic life, and AI-mediated institutions, then AI literacy must become part of readiness—not as a luxury skill, but as a matter of educational justice.

Equity, democratization, and local voice

Bulathwela, Pérez-Ortiz, Holloway, and Shawe-Taylor (2021) ask whether AI can democratize education while warning that the first wave of AI in education may instead deepen inequality if driven by technological determinism. Their work is valuable because it holds promise and critique together: AI may personalize learning and expand access, but without participatory, transparent, human-centered design, it may produce new forms of exclusion and resource misallocation. This supports the dissertation’s argument that democratizing education requires the voices of those affected by technology, not only the expertise of designers, policymakers, and vendors.

Cariaga and colleagues (2025) add an important local perspective. Their study of barriers to AI integration in K–12 classrooms emphasizes that global technologies are always implemented in specific contexts marked by local values, infrastructure, language, teacher preparation, cultural expectations, and child-safety concerns. This reinforces the methodological choice of participatory action research. Students, teachers, families, and community partners must help define AI readiness and educational thresholds because AI integration that ignores local voice risks becoming another top-down reform imposed on communities.

Curriculum beyond algorithms and transition infrastructure

Şahin (2025) argues for a curriculum beyond algorithms: a human-centered educational model that cultivates creativity, critical thinking, ethical reasoning, emotional intelligence, interdisciplinary inquiry, culturally responsive learning, and human flourishing. This work supports one of the dissertation’s central claims: AI literacy should not be reduced to coding, prompt engineering, or technical fluency. A value-creating curriculum must help students decide when to use AI, when to question it, when to resist it, and how to direct it toward humane purposes.

Deckker, Sumanasekara, and Uludag (2025) are especially relevant to the dissertation’s focus on bridge programs. They conceptualize AI as a form of “transitional infrastructure” connecting formal education with changing labor markets. Their review identifies AI-enhanced personalized learning, intelligent career guidance, institutional transformation, digital inclusion, and pedagogical-ethical considerations as central themes. This supports the dissertation’s argument that community-based bridge programs can become human-AI transition infrastructure: spaces where students receive relational guidance while using AI for college navigation, career exploration, apprenticeship matching, project development, and civic participation.

Synthesis of the literature

Taken together, this literature suggests that AI-era education should be organized around humanizing sociotechnical transition. AI should not be framed simply as a classroom aid, cheating threat, productivity enhancer, or future job skill. It should be understood as a social technology that reorganizes relationships among knowledge, labor, governance, identity, and opportunity.

Three standards follow from this synthesis:

  1. A humanizing standard: Does AI use strengthen dignity, agency, dialogue, creativity, care, and teacher-student relationships?
  1. An equity standard: Does AI use expand opportunity for marginalized students, or does it reproduce digital divides, surveillance, bias, and policy neglect?
  1. A transition standard: Does AI use help students cross real thresholds into college, apprenticeship, work, civic participation, and meaningful adult life?

These standards map directly onto Ikeda’s framework. Wisdom asks whether students understand AI as part of interconnected social systems. Courage asks whether they can engage uncertainty, challenge algorithmic authority, and pursue new pathways. Compassion asks whether educational redesign protects those most vulnerable to exclusion and helps every student create value.

Research questions

Primary research question: How can community-based bridge programs help reimagine compulsory secondary education as a value-creating, threshold-based pathway system for global citizenship in the age of agentic AI?

Secondary questions:

  1. How do students experience conventional high school structures in relation to agency, belonging, readiness, and future opportunity?
  2. How do students, educators, and community partners define readiness for postsecondary transition in a rapidly changing AI-shaped economy?
  3. How can Ikeda’s framework of wisdom, courage, and compassion help evaluate educational redesign?
  4. What role should AI literacy and human-AI collaboration play in secondary education?
  5. How might community-based bridge programs disrupt the school-to-prison pipeline by creating meaningful pathways into college, apprenticeship, work, and civic life?
  6. What kinds of educational thresholds should replace or supplement seat-time requirements?
  7. How can AI be integrated without weakening teacher agency, student dignity, local voice, or community accountability?
  8. What forms of governance, mentorship, and participatory design are needed for AI to democratize rather than stratify educational opportunity?

Proposed conceptual framework

The study uses a five-part conceptual framework.

  1. Value creation provides the purpose of education. Students should be prepared not merely to compete, comply, or credential, but to create value for themselves, their communities, and the wider world.
  2. Global citizenship provides the ethical orientation. Students should develop wisdom, courage, and compassion.
  3. Participatory action research provides the democratic method. Students and community partners should participate in defining problems, interpreting experiences, and designing solutions.
  4. Agentic AI provides the historical disruption. AI forces educators to reconsider what human learning is for when machines can increasingly perform routine cognitive labor.
  5. Humanizing sociotechnical design provides the implementation principle. AI should be integrated only in ways that strengthen agency, dignity, equity, relational trust, ethical judgment, and community contribution.

Methodological direction

This study will use participatory action research. The goal is not only to study students and educational institutions, but to work with participants to identify problems, interpret experiences, and imagine more humane educational pathways. Possible methods include interviews, focus groups, observations, student pathway mapping, document analysis, participatory coding sessions, and collaborative design workshops.

The study may focus on a Title I high school, a postsecondary advising program, a community-based bridge program, or a partnership among a school, community organization, college, and workforce partner. Participants may include high school students, recent graduates, postsecondary instructors, counselors, community organization staff, college access professionals, apprenticeship partners, workforce-development staff, and families.

Potential readiness thresholds

A threshold-based transition model would not simply ask, “Has the student completed four years of high school?” It would ask: Has the student demonstrated the wisdom, courage, compassion, and practical readiness necessary to move into a supported next pathway?

  • Academic literacy and numeracy
  • AI literacy and human-AI collaboration
  • Ethical reasoning and civic responsibility
  • Self-advocacy and communication
  • Financial and institutional navigation
  • Career exploration and practical skill development
  • Community contribution
  • Restorative conflict navigation
  • Postsecondary pathway planning
  • Mentorship and support networks
  • Local and community accountability

Working contribution

This dissertation contributes to value-creating education by extending it into the context of agentic AI, workforce transformation, and secondary school redesign. It also contributes to critical pedagogy by arguing that students need not only critical consciousness, but also practical pathways into meaningful participation.

Public education should prepare students not merely to survive automation, but to participate in shaping humane technological futures. In the age of agentic AI, the central purpose of education must shift from information transmission to value creation.
